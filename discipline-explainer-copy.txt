STAGE 2 — INVESTIGATION & REVIEW
================================

[stage label]
Investigation and review

[box, left track]
Department investigation

[copy under that box]
Supervisors investigate and record facts, findings and applicable policy
violations. Cases enter discipline-tracking software and move through the
chain of command to the police chief or safety director. At each step,
supervisors review whether the alleged violations were properly identified and
investigated. They can request more information, correct details, or add,
remove or change alleged violations.

[on phones only, the public complaint box reappears here feeding the
 right-hand track — repeated from stage 1]
A member of the public files a complaint

[box, right track]
External investigation

[copy under that box — "The Office of Professional Standards" is bold]
The Office of Professional Standards, a civilian office outside the police
department, investigates noncriminal complaints from members of the public.

[continuing, same column — "Civilian Police Review Board" is bold]
The Office of Professional Standards sends its findings and recommendations
to the Civilian Police Review Board, an independent group of residents
appointed by the mayor and City Council.

The board reviews the investigation and makes its findings at a public
meeting. Possible findings for each allegation include:

   Sustained: The evidence supports the allegation.
   Unfounded: The alleged conduct did not occur.
   Insufficient evidence: There is not enough evidence to prove or
   disprove the allegation.
   Exonerated: The conduct occurred but did not violate policy.

If the board finds a violation and recommends discipline, the employee gets
an opportunity to respond at a discipline hearing.

[subheading, centred below both tracks — appears, then clears]
Internal Affairs Unit investigates possible crimes and more serious
allegations of misconduct.

[copy under that subheading]
A prosecutor reviews the criminal investigation and decides whether to file
charges.

Criminal charges and department discipline must be handled separately. An
employee can face discipline for violating policy even if no criminal charges
are filed. The discipline process can also proceed while criminal charges are
pending.

[box]
Chief of Police review and charging letter

[copy under that box]
In most cases, the police chief reviews the investigation and decides whether
the evidence supports the violation. The most serious violations go directly
to the safety director.

The case can be sent back for more investigation if something was missed. At
this point, lower-level misconduct may be resolved with verbal counseling.
Those cases never enter the formal disciplinary process.

The chief or safety director begins the formal disciplinary process by sending
the case to the Case Preparation Office. That office schedules the pre-
disciplinary hearing and sends a “charge letter” to the employee that outlines
the city's account and the alleged policy violations.

Under the collective bargaining agreement, discipline must be issued within 90
days of the original hearing notice. After 90 days, no discipline can be
imposed.


STAGE 3 — DISCIPLINARY HEARING
==============================

[stage label]
Disciplinary hearing

[box]
Pre-disciplinary hearing

[copy under the box]
Before discipline is imposed, the employee can respond with evidence,
defenses or witnesses. The employee can also waive the hearing.

The hearing officer — usually a deputy chief or assistant public safety
director — reviews the evidence to decide whether it is more likely than not
that misconduct occurred. For complaints from the public, the Civilian Police
Review Board presents its findings and recommendations to the hearing officer.


STAGE 4 — DISCIPLINE DECISION
=============================

[stage label]
Discipline decision

[box]
Corrective action

[subheading]
Who decides the consequences?

[copy]
If an employee is found to have violated policy, officials determine the
consequence. The city calls this "corrective action," which includes both
formal discipline and steps that are not considered discipline.

The chief generally decides cases involving suspensions of 10 days or less.
The public safety director decides cases that could result in longer
suspensions or termination.

["For Civilian Police Review Board cases" is bold]
For Civilian Police Review Board cases, the chief must follow the board's
findings and recommendations, under the city charter. If the chief disagrees
with the review board, they send what's called a “departure memo” explaining
their reasoning. That could be a misread policy or a conflicting General
Police Order.

Then, the board rehears the case, considering the memo, and issues its final
decision. The board's decision is final, and the chief must issue that
discipline.

for different violations — to guide these decisions.

 chart, so any change applies twice]

[the three groups — repeated in the phone version, so any change applies
 twice]
GROUP I
Less serious violations
that generally have a limited impact on the department or public, such as
tardiness, discourtesy or some reporting errors.

GROUP II
More serious violations
that interfere with the department's mission or create significant risks to
public or officer safety.

GROUP III
The most serious violations
including serious abuses of authority, unethical conduct, excessive force,
dishonesty, discrimination and some criminal offenses. All Group 3 cases go to
the Safety Director.

[copy below the chart]
The groups have general ranges for discipline so that employees are treated
consistently. Supervisors making discipline decisions can also consider:

   The seriousness of the misconduct
   Prior disciplinary history back to a certain point
   Aggravating factors that may justify a harsher penalty. That could
   include dishonesty, harm to the public or repeated misconduct.
   Mitigating factors that may support a lesser penalty. That could include
   accepting responsibility or lack of previous discipline.

STAGE 5 — CHALLENGING DISCIPLINE
================================

[stage label]
Challenging discipline

[subheading]
How employees can challenge discipline

[three boxes in a small diagram: Grievance leads down to Arbitration, with
 a dotted line out to the side to Settlement]
Grievance
Settlement
Arbitration

[copy beside the diagram]
An officer or other union-represented employee can challenge discipline by
filing a grievance.

The city has a labor relations office, which considers the grievance arguments
and consults with the law department on a potential resolution, called a
settlement.

If they cannot reach an agreement, the union may take the case to arbitration.

In arbitration, a neutral third party holds a hearing, reviews evidence and
issues a written decision that both sides agree to follow. Only in rare
instances can that be appealed to a local court.

It is unclear how often grievances lead to settlements or changes in
discipline. Settlements and arbitration decisions are public records but are
not published by the city like discipline decisions are.

[subheading]
Where the Community Police Commission fits in

[box]
Direct appeal

[copy under the box]
The city charter gives Cleveland's Community Police Commission final
authority within city government over police discipline. Voters expanded its
powers by passing Issue 24 in 2021.

The commission can order the chief and public safety director to impose or
increase discipline and, in limited circumstances, decrease it.

A resident or officer who disagrees with a discipline decision can petition
the commission for review. They can make the request at a public meeting,
submit it in writing or ask a commissioner to submit it on their behalf.

Commission members publicly discuss and vote on whether to review a case. If
they agree to proceed, the next step is to hear evidence.

How and when the commission can intervene remains disputed, in part because
union contracts set deadlines for discipline. The unions argue that the
commission's charter powers do not override state collective bargaining law.

The commission has proposed policy changes that would allow it to get
involved earlier in discipline cases to address those timing disputes. The
revised policy is not yet in effect.
